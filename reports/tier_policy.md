# Which scheduler for which tier

_Generated by `scripts/tier_policy.py` from the shipped benchmark._

The optimum moves with emitter density, and it moves in the direction the
physics predicts. A receiver seeing `K` of `B` channels covers the band on a
fixed revisit period; whether that suffices depends on how many emitters must
be found in it.

- **EASY** — 5 emitters. Coverage is nearly free: every sweep and bandit reaches
  zero never-intercepted, so the constraint is slack and the slot budget can go
  to exploitation.
- **MEDIUM** — 15 emitters. The constraint starts to bind; the frontier has a
  real trade-off along it.
- **HARD** — 30 emitters, 5 decoys. Coverage is the binding constraint. Every
  slot a predictor takes is a slot not spent finding an emitter nobody has seen,
  and emitter channels are drawn uniformly at random, so before first contact
  there is nothing to predict.

| **`predictor`** | 5 | 0.14187 | 0.0470 | 1.000 |
| **`ppo`** | 8 | 0.19160 | 0.0470 | 1.000 |

_Pareto-optimal over (never-intercepted, TWIR) from 13 schedulers, 30 seeds. Everything else is beaten on both axes._

- **Find everything once** → `epsilon_greedy` (0 never intercepted)
- **Collect the most from what is found** → `ppo` (TWIR 0.19160, 8 never intercepted)
- The price of the swap: **+8 emitters never intercepted** for **2.9x the TWIR**.

## Reading this as one strategy

The pattern across tiers is the actual result: **as emitter density rises, the
optimal policy shifts from exploitation to coverage.** That is a scan strategy
that adapts to the environment rather than a fixed schedule, which is what the
problem statement asks for -- and the switch can be driven online, since the
count of distinct emitters found in the first second is an observable the
receiver already has.
